**Who paid the 2025–26 tariffs? I measured it product by product.**

Using monthly US Census import data for every product and country (3.7 million product × country ×
month observations), I tested whether foreign exporters cut their prices when US tariffs rose.

For the most part, they didn't.

📦 **At the border:** pre-duty import prices barely moved. Of each $1 of duty, roughly 96–100 cents was added to what US importers paid. The 2018–19 trade war produced the same result, and I reproduce it with the same data and method.

🔩 **The exception:** for Section 232 metals and autos, exporters cut prices by about 9% of the duty.

🏷️ **In stores:** consumer prices of the most exposed goods rose by roughly 30–65% of the duty cost of their imports, gradually. These estimates are imprecise.

📉 **On paper vs in practice:** the average tariff collected peaked at 10.8% (October 2025), against 16.8% on paper. USMCA claims, in-transit exemptions and duties on metal content only explain the gap.

🌏 **Trade shifted:** China's share of US imports of the products tariffed in 2018–19 fell from 25.5% to 7.4%, with Taiwan, Vietnam, Mexico and Southeast Asia gaining.

What this doesn't say: nothing about overall welfare or whether the policy was right. It measures prices and trade flows.

Everything is open. Every tariff rate in the timeline is cited to the Federal Register or a proclamation, and the code, data and tests are on GitHub. The site updates monthly as Census releases new data.

You can also look up any product ("washing machines", "solar panels", "toys") in the explorer to see its tariffs, prices and suppliers over time.
